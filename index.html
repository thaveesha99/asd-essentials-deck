Nothing you did under Essential Eight is wasted — every mitigation strategy maps to at least one principle. But you cannot relabel yesterday's maturity level and claim the same result.

Three questions everything hangs off

Principle
What outcome am I trying to achieve?
e.g. identity and access are managed so that access is controlled and misuse is detectable
Action
What do I actually need to do?
e.g. use phishing-resistant MFA for users authenticating to systems
Domain
Where does it apply?
e.g. Identity services, Boundary services, Privileged environments
The third question is the shift. “We do MFA” is no longer an organisation-wide claim — it is judged separately in each area, and you will be strong in some and weak in others.

The principle is the constant. The actions are the variable. PRO-13 keeps its text and its ISM identifier in every chapter, while the actions underneath it are rewritten for what the environment allows you to do.
The principle also keeps its identifier across domains — but which actions apply, and how they rate, changes with the assets in scope.

Do not map these onto the org chart. “This division is a domain” is wrong. Domains group by what function a system performs, not who owns it. A subsidiary is a separate environment with its own eight domains.

A to-do order, not a maturity level. “We’re at phase 2” means we have started work on these areas — not that you are more secure than a well-run organisation still on phase 1. Your position comes only from the ratings.
Later phases still have to be done. Changing the order changes what happens first, not what has to happen.

How you are scored

Not assessed
We have not looked yet. Neutral — not a failure. Must always be reported separately from “not effective”.
Not effective
Missing, or present but achieving nothing useful. Effort and spend are irrelevant.
Partially effective
Genuinely reduces risk, but with gaps in coverage or consistency.
Fully effective
Works across the whole area, and we can prove it.
Each domain then gets a percentage: fully = 1, partially = 0.5, not effective = 0, divided by applicable principles. Not assessed is excluded from the maths — which is why it must be reported alongside.

The framework on one page

The same eight domains in every chapter; the sequence shown is enterprise IT and cloud. In OT, Boundary services and Critical processes come first.

Two rules that catch people out

Weakest link wins

A principle is rated by its worst action. Four actions fully effective and one not effective means the whole principle is not effective.

Early scores look harsh. Brief your executives before the first report, or the programme will look like it is failing when it is not.

Starting work does not count

A plan, a purchase order, or a tool on half the estate is not “partially effective”.

Partial credit requires a real reduction in risk. “We bought it” is not a result.

Partial activity is not partial effectiveness. ASD repeats this in every publication. It is the single most common way organisations over-report their own posture.

Worked example

One domain,
end to end

Northgate Water — a 2,000-person utility. Illustrative figures throughout.

The domain: Identity services

What is in scope

Domain controllers, Entra ID / AD
Federation servers, certificate authorities
Credential stores and password managers
The HR system that feeds accounts into the directory
The test: does the system’s identity function have effect beyond itself? A system with its own user accounts is not an identity service. One that authenticates users into other systems is.

Why it comes first

Compromise gives broad, cascading access. An adversary who controls the directory can impersonate users, escalate privileges and move laterally while bypassing every control that treats identity as a trust signal.

That is why it sits in phase 1 alongside Boundary services.

The principle: PRO-13

Robust and secure identity, credential and access management is used to establish, maintain and control access to systems, and to support effective detection of identity and credential misuse. Identity, credential and access management — ISM principle GOV/PRO-13
Note what the principle is not: it is not “have MFA”. It is the outcome — access is controlled and misuse is detectable. That framing is why the actions underneath it do not all look like MFA.

Part two

Three environments,
one model

Enterprise IT, cloud and OT are parallel chapters — not a ladder you climb.

Which chapter applies

ChapterCoversTrigger
Enterprise ITOn-premises, hybrid, IaaSYou still own the OS, applications, identity and network
CloudEnvironments delivered entirely as SaaS and PaaSYou configure services you do not operate
OTSystems that cause a direct change to the physical environmentYou control plant, process or safety systems

IaaS is not “cloud” here. Renting virtual servers still leaves you patching, hardening and owning the network design — that is enterprise IT work.

The cloud chapter is deliberately narrow. It is written for organisations whose environment is delivered entirely as SaaS and PaaS.

“There is no requirement to work through them in a set order. The chapters are designed to be applied independently, in the sequence that best reflects an organisation’s environments, obligations and risk.”

The awkward middle

ASD’s own admission, and where most real estates live.

SaaS behaving like a platform

Low-code development, scripting, automation or private networking mean the service is doing platform work. Apply the PaaS actions — even though you bought it as SaaS.

PaaS drifting towards IaaS

Direct control of compute or networking means you are carrying IaaS responsibilities. Consider whether the enterprise IT chapter fits better.

“Service model boundaries are a matter of degree, and organisations should make a considered judgement about what they actually operate rather than what the procurement label says.” The contract does not decide which chapter applies. What you actually operate does.

Cloud: who does what

C — Customer
You build and run it. Assessed on your control.
S — Supplied
The provider gives the capability; you must enable, configure, operate and monitor it.
A capability left at an insecure default, or applied inconsistently, is not yet effective.
P — Provider
The provider does it. You must verify — with current independent assurance, such as IRAP scoped to the services, features, regions and support you actually consume.
The (S) rows are where organisations quietly carry risk they think they have outsourced. Markers are indicative, not determinative — and PaaS customers usually carry more responsibility than SaaS.

OT: safety outranks everything

Two definitions that do real work

OT — systems that detect or cause a direct change to the physical environment through monitoring or control of devices, processes and events.

Vital OT — the subset it is unacceptable to have offline for an extended period, judged against safe delivery of a minimum viable product.

That second definition sets exactly what isolation must protect.

The order changes

Phase 1Boundary services · Critical processes
Phase 2Privileged environments · Identity services
Phase 3Business systems

Because you often cannot patch or replace without affecting operations — so ASD leans on protecting critical processes, controlling connectivity, and legitimate compensating controls.

OT: devices, access and recovery

…should prohibit the use of typically configured end-user or endpoint devices within OT environments. ASD Essentials for OT — User devices domain

The User devices domain is excluded from OT’s technical matrix entirely. HMIs, engineering workstations and operator stations all attract privileged-environment controls. Budget for it.

Three principles that bite commercially

PRO-19DMZ at every OT perimeter; cables physically identified; isolation reversible only from inside vital OT; safe operation sustained for a defined period.
PRO-20Remote access terminates in the OT DMZ — never routed in. Time-bound grants, no permanent connections.
REC-02Recover vital systems without supplier support, including replacing key devices after a destructive attack. Your support contract is not a recovery plan.

Where the three meet

“Compromise in one environment frequently provides a pathway into another.”

Boundary servicesThe seam in every chapter. Enterprise IT scoping explicitly includes cloud service boundaries and OT boundaries.
IdentityThe shared trust anchor across all three environments.
Business systemsCan straddle — where they support OT processes, ASD counts them as part of the OT system.
OT dependenciesLicensing, activation, updates, supplier support, vendor cloud analytics. Vital OT must keep working — and keep logging — when everything outside it is unavailable.
Shared functionsScope once, spanning both environments. They usually carry a higher consequence of compromise.

AI is not a separate chapter

Threat
Attacks are faster, cheaper and open to more people — and AI adds new surface: prompt injection, tampered models, synthetic voice and video.
Solution
Useful for correlating events, triaging alerts and synthesising intelligence — but AI security products are themselves AI assets.
Asset
Models, training data, prompts, outputs, connectors and the non-person identities agents use — governed like any other asset.

Where AI is scoped in the domains

Supports a core organisational outcomeCritical processes
Embedded in a general business platformBusiness systems
Public-facing AI service, API or connectorBoundary services
Agent and its non-person identityIdentity services
Capable of administrative actionPrivileged environments
Assume prompt injection cannot be completely prevented — so contain it. A manipulated model must not exceed the access it has been granted, and safety systems must enforce their own limits.

What AI changes in each environment

Enterprise IT

Verify model weights and training data before use. Extend vulnerability management to prompt injection and model extraction. Approve AI tools before they reach organisational data. Log prompts, outputs and retrieval.

Cloud

Executive approval before enabling SaaS or PaaS AI functions. Record AI in the shared responsibility register. Give every agent an owned, constrained identity. Put tenant AI settings in the baseline.

Operational technology

Vital OT must not depend on AI outside OT. Constrain model outputs to safe limits, enforced independently. Route AI vendor services through the OT DMZ. Recover without supplier support.

The framework applies whether you consume, develop or deploy AI. ASD expects the core principles to stay stable while AI-specific guidance is updated in supporting publications.

A large gap is not, by itself, a reason to start somewhere. ASD weighs four questions together: what you operate, what you are required to do, where the biggest gap is, and where the greatest risk lies.
